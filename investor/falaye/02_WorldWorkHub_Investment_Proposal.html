The problem

Someone looking for work outside their own country does three frustrating things in sequence. They search general job boards that bury sponsorship in the description, or never mention it at all. They open dozens of tabs across aggregators, recruiters and government portals to compare roles that may not be comparable. And then they spend hours on applications where the employer was never in a position to sponsor anyone.

The information exists. It is scattered, unlabelled and inconsistent in format. The person doing the search pays for that with weeks of their life, and the employer pays for it by receiving applications from candidates they cannot legally hire.

The product

  • A single search surface. The extension pulls listings from multiple sources and presents them as cards inside the browser, without a tab-switching workflow.
  • Filters that match the real question. Visa sponsorship, remote or onsite or hybrid, employment type, industry, and country — served from the backend rather than hard-coded in the popup.
  • Query-scoped counts. The user sees how many results their search returned, and what the free tier unlocks, rather than a vanity total for the whole database.
  • A sponsorship signal, not a promise. Where a listing indicates sponsorship, it is surfaced as a signal. It is never presented as an entitlement, and the extension says so.
  • Saving and tracking. Favourites and a shortlist so a search across three weeks does not become a folder of lost links.
  • The original source, always. Every listing links back to the employer or board. We do not intermediate applications we cannot support.

Why the number went down, not up. The database once held roughly 95,000 records. A large share were duplicates, expired postings, or listings whose source could not be named. I removed them. A smaller number I can defend is worth more than a larger number I cannot — especially in a product whose entire promise is that the user can trust what they are looking at. Any investor who prefers the bigger number is buying a marketing claim, not a database.

Market

The direction of travel is not controversial: more roles are remote, more employers hire across borders, and fewer candidates know how to find the ones that will sponsor. The figures below were used in my earlier deck and are industry estimates rather than our own research; they should be re-verified before any external publication.

  • Around three in four employers report difficulty filling roles — the pressure that pushes hiring across borders.
  • Roughly one in four workers now wants fully remote work, which widens the pool an employer can consider.
  • Recruitment software is projected to be a multi-billion-dollar market by 2030, with sponsorship and mobility among the least-served segments.

What I can claim as our own is narrower and more useful: the sponsorship question is the bottleneck for the user, and no mainstream job board treats it as a first-class filter. That is the gap the product occupies.

The one-sentence version. WorldWorkHub is past the idea stage but short of proof: the product exists, the channel works, the data needs auditing and nobody has paid yet. ₦30m in three gated tranches buys twelve months to convert that into measured users and revenue — or clear evidence to stop.
